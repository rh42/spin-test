The Spin Test — Spatial Permutation for Cortical Maps

The spin test (Alexander-Bloch et al., 2018, NeuroImage) is a null model for testing whether two cortical maps are correlated beyond what spatial autocorrelation alone predicts. Random rotations are applied to a spherical representation of the cortical surface, preserving spatial smoothness while destroying anatomical alignment. This interactive demo lets you run 1000 random spins and watch the null distribution build in real time.

What am I looking at?

A brain map, having an identity crisis.

Neuroscientists often want to know if two brain features line up. Say, does myelin — the fatty coating that speeds up signals — follow the same pattern as which network a region belongs to? Match or no match, that's worth knowing.

Obvious move: correlate the two maps. Done? Not quite. The brain is smooth — nearby regions resemble each other just for being neighbours. So two unrelated maps still overlap, because they're painted on the same smeared-out blob. The brain isn't lying to you. It's just being the brain.

So how do you know a match is real? You build a pile of fake ones to beat. Take one map and spin it to a random angle on the sphere — not the brain, just the map, like scribbling "myelin" on a globe and giving it a flick. Now it's misaligned with the other map, but still internally smooth. Measure the correlation. That's one fake match. Do it a thousand times.

The orange line is the correlation you actually measured, properly aligned. Swallowed by the pile? Smoothness was doing the work. Standing clear of it? Something real might be going on.

That's the spin test. (Nobody in my lab had heard of it either.)
